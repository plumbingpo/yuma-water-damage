Structural drying is the most important phase of water damage restoration, and the one untrained responders most often get wrong. Once standing water has been extracted, a great deal of moisture is still held inside the structure: in insulation, concrete, subfloors, wood framing, drywall and other components. This trapped moisture must be scientifically removed through controlled evaporation and dehumidification to prevent the mold growth, wood rot, material degradation and odor problems that develop when structures remain wet.

Psychrometry is the science of how air temperature, humidity and moisture interact, and the IICRC-certified technicians who dry structures for Yuma Restoration Co know it well. Using this knowledge, they calculate the specific equipment placement, temperature settings and airflow patterns needed to create the right evaporation conditions for each drying situation. Plaster, concrete, hardwood and drywall each dry at their own rate, so each needs its own approach to get down to an acceptable moisture level with no added damage.

Our structural drying process includes daily moisture monitoring with calibrated moisture meters, thermo-hygrometers and thermal imaging cameras. The result is a detailed log of readings that tracks how moisture comes down over time in each material that got wet. Each day we use those numbers to reposition or retune the equipment so drying stays efficient. A surface that feels dry to your hand proves nothing, so the job is finished only once all affected materials test within the acceptable standards set by IICRC S500.

Signs You Need Structural Drying in Yuma

Recognizing the warning signs early can save you time, money, and prevent significant damage to your property. Watch for these telltale signs that professional help is needed:

  • Humidity that will not come down in the affected space, whatever ventilation you try
  • No visible water left, yet walls or floors still feel cold and damp
  • After water damage, a meter shows elevated moisture in the floor, wall or ceiling
  • Wood flooring with boards that cup, crown or pull apart
  • Earlier water damage that was never professionally dried and now shows deterioration
  • Swollen trim and baseboards separating at the seams
  • Wall finishes that start to peel or bubble in the days after water damage

Why Professional Structural Drying Matters

Structural drying is a science, not a guessing game. Simply placing fans and hoping for the best is the most common cause of secondary mold damage after water events. Professional structural drying requires understanding how different materials absorb and release moisture, calculating the grain depression (moisture removal capacity) each situation needs, and monitoring daily to confirm drying is progressing properly. Without this knowledge, under-drying leaves moisture that feeds mold, while over-drying can damage wood and other materials.

Professional drying equipment is not a bigger version of what stores sell, it is a different kind of machine. A commercial LGR dehumidifier, short for low-grain refrigerant, pulls 10 to 20 times as much moisture out of the air as a residential one, while high-velocity air movers set up the exact airflow that speeds evaporation off structural surfaces. Between the investment in equipment and the training needed to run it, it is no surprise that DIY drying falls well short of a professional job.

How to Prevent Structural Drying Problems

Taking preventive measures can help you avoid costly water damage issues down the road. Here are our expert recommendations:

  • Deal with water intrusion right away, since an early start on drying gives a better result
  • Until a technician removes the equipment, keep it running day and night without breaks
  • Shut the doors and windows of any room under drying, which keeps dehumidification working well
  • Daily monitoring only works if someone can let the technician into the property
  • Do not place personal items on top of drying equipment or block the airflow from air movers
  • Go by the meter, not by touch, since a dry-feeling surface may still hold moisture deep inside
